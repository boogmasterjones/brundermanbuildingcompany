Port Charlotte and North Port were both laid out by General Development Corporation beginning in the 1950s: mile after mile of streets and canals, divided into lots of roughly 80 by 125 feet, most of which sat empty for decades. Many are owned by people who bought them long ago or inherited them. If that is you, the first thing to understand is that the lot may be the least expensive part of your future home — but it is also the part with the most unknowns. Two lots on the same street can differ by tens of thousands of dollars in site costs.

## 1\. Flood Zone and Required Floor Elevation

Look up the lot on the FEMA flood map or your county's GIS site. If it is in a special flood hazard area, the map gives a base flood elevation, and local rules typically require the finished floor to sit above it by a set margin. The difference between the existing ground and the required floor height tells you how much fill, stem wall, or elevated construction the house will need.

## 2\. Fill and Drainage

Even outside a flood zone, most lots need fill to raise the building pad above the road and direct water to the swales. Low or wet lots need more. Fill dirt, compaction, and grading are real line items, and they have to be placed without pushing stormwater onto the neighbors.

## 3\. Water and Sewer — or Well and Septic

Utility service is block by block. Some areas have central water and sewer, some have water only, and many — especially in North Port — have neither. Without utilities you will need a drilled well with treatment equipment and a permitted septic system. The septic design depends on soil and the seasonal high water table, and the tank and drain field occupy a meaningful part of the lot, which affects where the house, driveway, and a future pool can go.

## 4\. Protected Species

Two protected animals come up constantly here. Gopher tortoise burrows must be surveyed for, and any tortoises relocated under a state permit before clearing. Parts of Charlotte County and North Port are also mapped Florida scrub-jay habitat, where building may involve a review and a mitigation fee. Neither is a deal-breaker, but both take time, so check early.

## 5\. Setbacks, Easements, and Deed Restrictions

A current survey shows the lot lines, easements, and any encroachments. Zoning sets the front, side, and rear setbacks; canal lots have additional setbacks from the water. Some subdivisions carry deed restrictions on minimum home size or exterior materials. Together these define the buildable envelope — know it before you fall in love with a floor plan.

## 6\. Canal Lots: Seawalls and Access

On a saltwater canal, find out the condition of the seawall and whether the route to open water passes under fixed bridges. A failing seawall is a major expense best handled before the house is built, while equipment can still reach it.

## 7\. Impact Fees and Permit Costs

Charlotte County and the City of North Port both charge impact fees on new homes, in addition to permit fees. They change periodically, so ask your builder for current figures and make sure they are in the budget rather than a surprise at permit pickup.
