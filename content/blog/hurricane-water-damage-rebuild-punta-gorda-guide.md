After a storm, every homeowner in Punta Gorda, Port Charlotte and the rest of Charlotte County wants the same thing: to get back into a safe, dry house as soon as possible. The quickest-looking repair isn't always the one that holds up, and it isn't always the one the building department will approve.

Brunderman Building Co Inc has been building and rebuilding homes in this area since Brian Brunderman founded the company in 1987. Here is the practical order a hurricane or water damage rebuild usually follows, and the decisions worth making along the way.

## Step 1: Make It Safe and Document Everything

Before anyone talks about cabinets or flooring, the house has to be safe to enter and protected from further damage. That usually means:

- Checking for structural damage, downed lines and electrical hazards before going inside
- Covering roof openings and broken windows to keep more water out
- Getting standing water and wet materials out quickly, because moisture keeps spreading into walls, subfloors and insulation
- Photographing and videoing every room, inside and out, before anything is removed

Good documentation helps you, your insurance company and your builder agree on what was damaged. Brunderman Building Co Inc does not handle insurance claims, so talk with your insurer directly about coverage. A builder can still help you understand what needs repair and what a rebuild will involve.

## Step 2: Get a Full Damage Assessment

Water damage is often worse than it looks. Drywall that seems dry on the surface can be wet inside the wall cavity, and a roof leak can run along framing and show up far from where it entered.

A proper assessment looks at the structure, roof, windows and doors, electrical, plumbing, and interior finishes. On concrete block homes, it also means checking how the roof connects to the walls, since that connection is a big part of how a house handles wind.

This is also the point where the scope of the project starts to take shape, and where one federal rule can change it a lot.

## Step 3: Understand the FEMA 50% Substantial Damage Rule

If your home is in a flood zone, the FEMA 50% rule can decide what kind of project you're actually doing. In general terms, when the cost to repair a damaged building reaches 50% or more of the structure's market value, the local building department can determine it is "substantially damaged." At that point, the home may have to be brought up to current floodplain requirements instead of simply being repaired to how it was.

That can mean a very different project: possibly elevating the home or rebuilding to current standards, rather than replacing drywall and flooring. Many homeowners in Punta Gorda Isles, Port Charlotte and other canal-front neighborhoods find this out only after the repair estimate comes in.

Knowing where you stand early helps you plan. We explain the rule in more detail in our guide to [the FEMA 50% rule in Charlotte County](/blog/fema-50-percent-rule-charlotte-county).

## Step 4: Plan the Rebuild and Pull Permits

It's tempting to skip permits after a storm, especially when contractors are knocking on doors offering to start tomorrow. Unpermitted storm repairs can cause real trouble:

- Work that doesn't meet current code may have to be torn out and redone
- Unpermitted work can create problems when you sell or refinance
- Without inspections, nobody independent has checked that the structural, electrical and plumbing work was done correctly

Permits are also how the building department tracks substantial damage determinations, so working with them, not around them, protects you. Brunderman Building Co Inc coordinates permits, subcontractors and inspections under one contract, so you have one point of contact instead of chasing several trades and the permit office on your own.

## Step 5: Use the Rebuild to Harden the House

A rebuild is the best time to make your home stronger for the next storm. Walls are already open, windows may already be out, and the roof may already be coming off. Adding protection now usually takes far less disruption than doing it later as a separate project.

Upgrades worth discussing include:

- **Impact-rated windows and doors.** These are standard on every new home Brunderman builds, and replacing storm-damaged openings with impact-rated units means you don't have to put up shutters every time a storm heads this way.
- **A continuous load path.** Brunderman builds new homes with concrete block and a continuous load path, which ties the roof, walls and foundation together so wind loads move down through the structure. During a rebuild, it's worth looking at how well your existing connections do this.
- **Water-resistant finishes.** Choosing flooring and lower-wall materials that tolerate moisture better can make future cleanup easier.
- **Layout improvements.** If the interior is being rebuilt anyway, some homeowners take the chance to open up the floor plan, add a walk-in shower or make the home easier to live in long term.

## Step 6: Construction and Inspections

Once permits are issued, the work typically moves from structural repairs to roofing and drying-in, then windows and doors, then rough electrical, plumbing and mechanical, then insulation, drywall and finishes. Inspections happen at set stages. A failed inspection means fixing the work before moving on, which is one more reason to have one contractor coordinating the schedule.

## Step 7: Final Inspection and Moving Back In

The project wraps up with final inspections and sign-off from the building department. Keep copies of your permits, final inspection records and product information for impact-rated windows and doors. They're useful for your records, for future buyers, and for any conversation with your insurer.
