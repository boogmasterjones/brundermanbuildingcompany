Florida has a single statewide building code, but permits are issued locally — by Charlotte County or Sarasota County, or by cities such as Punta Gorda, North Port, and Venice. The details differ a little from one counter to the next, yet the basic principle is the same everywhere: work that affects structure, life safety, or the building's systems needs a permit; purely cosmetic work generally does not.

## Work That Generally Needs a Permit

-   Removing, moving, or adding walls — especially anything load-bearing
-   New or relocated plumbing: moving a sink, converting a tub to a shower, adding a bathroom
-   New circuits, panel upgrades, and most electrical work beyond like-for-like device replacement
-   HVAC replacement or new ductwork
-   Window and exterior door replacement (products must meet wind-load and, in our region, impact or shutter requirements)
-   Re-roofing and structural roof repairs
-   Room additions, lanai enclosures, and garage conversions
-   Water heater replacement in most jurisdictions

## Work That Usually Does Not

-   Interior and exterior painting
-   Flooring replacement
-   Cabinet and countertop replacement in the same layout, with no plumbing or electrical changes
-   Replacing a faucet, toilet, or light fixture in the same location
-   Minor drywall repair and trim work

The gray area is where most kitchen and bath projects live. Swapping cabinets is cosmetic; adding an island with outlets and a prep sink is not. Re-tiling a shower may not need a permit; replacing the valve, moving the drain, or changing the footprint does. When in doubt, a quick call to the building department settles it.

## Why Permits Are Worth It

**Inspections catch what you cannot see.** A second set of trained eyes on wiring, plumbing, and structure before the walls close is inexpensive insurance.

**Unpermitted work surfaces at the worst time.** Home inspectors, buyers' agents, and insurers routinely compare a house against its permit history. Open or missing permits can delay a sale, reduce an offer, or complicate a claim.

**In a flood zone, it is the only way to document compliance.** Permit records establish what was spent and when, which matters under the [FEMA 50% rule](/blog/fema-50-percent-rule-charlotte-county).

## Who Pulls the Permit?

When you hire a licensed contractor, the contractor normally applies for the permit and is responsible for the work passing inspection. Florida does allow owner-builder permits for your own residence, but the owner then takes on the legal responsibility a contractor would carry, including supervising the work and using properly licensed trades. Be cautious of anyone who asks you to pull an owner-builder permit for work they will be doing.

## How Long Does It Take?

Simple trade permits are often issued quickly; projects requiring plan review take longer, and timelines stretch after major storms when departments are flooded with applications. A complete, well-prepared submittal is the best way to avoid delays.
